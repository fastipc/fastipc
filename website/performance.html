Performance

Every number here comes from the repository's benchmark suites, run on one laptop (the macOS and Linux VM columns on a MacBook Air): two processes, one sending and one receiving, connected through the public API. Linux numbers are WSL2 on the same machine as Windows', and Linux in a VM on the MacBook. Treat them as a guide to the shape of the costs; your hardware will differ.

Lowest latency: busy-polling

A receiver that waits in fipc_recv spins for 100 to 250 µs, then sleeps; a message that comes later wakes it in tens of microseconds. For the lowest latency at any message rate, poll instead: call fipc_recv (or fipc_rpc_recv, fipc_recv_acquire, or a binding's receive) with timeout 0 in a loop, and call again on FIPC_TIMEOUT. A call that finds nothing makes no system call and reads no clock: it looks at the ring's indices and the connection's state and returns, in about 20 ns. The polling thread holds one core at 100%.

Reading the numbers

what the docs say about them
16–256 B, copy vs zero-copy

The receiver sets the pace

Short copied messages cost about as much per message as zero-copy ones. But the copying benchmark's receiver keeps up with its sender, so every publish would wait about 50 ns for cache lines to return from the reader's core. A receiver that waits for a stream therefore holds back its first looks (64 pauses on Windows, 32 on Linux, 256 on Apple Silicon), which lifts copy and RPC at 16 to 64 B 1.4 to 2.4 times; one that waits for an answer looks at once. The pace is a build option: zig build -Dstream_pace=N builds another for other hardware. A receiver that does real work on its messages runs in the faster regime.

ARM64, 256 B–1.5 KiB

Don't poll a stream too eagerly

A receiver that had caught up with a stream used to look at the ring after every spin hint, and on the M1 that held 1 KiB messages at about 8M per second. Measured: such a receiver caught up at most messages and slowed its sender, so it stayed caught up. Inferred, not measured: each look takes the 128-byte cache line of the ring's indices from the sender, which must win it back for its next message. A receiver that only receives now waits about 2 µs before its next look on ARM64: 1 KiB runs at 25 to 27M per second under macOS and Linux, and the round trip's p50 is unchanged, because a side waiting for an answer still looks after every hint.

noise

How much runs differ

From 1 KiB up, two runs of the same library agree within 2 to 3%. At 16 to 64 B they differ by up to 4% on Windows and 9% on WSL. The round trip's p50 holds within 1% (on WSL a round's lands near 240 or near 270 ns, so compare many rounds), setup within 1%.

latency-sleep · wakeup

Waking a sleeper

When the receiver has gone to sleep, a message reaches it in about 35 to 45 µs on Windows (some runs near 100 µs), 50 to 65 µs on WSL and 26 µs on macOS, depending on how deeply its core slept. A waiting side spins for 100 to 250 µs before it sleeps.

64 KiB and up

Bandwidth

At 64 KiB the copy runs at 30 GiB/s on Linux and 26 GiB/s on Windows. Messages longer than the ring go in pieces, and copy, zero-copy and RPC then run alike.

Windows throttling · macOS background

Quality of service

The suite turns Windows power throttling off for its processes, and devtool for every process the other languages' benchmarks start. Started from a background process otherwise, Windows 11 runs them on efficiency cores: 64 KiB messages fall to 210 to 280K per second. macOS has its counterpart, the background state, which runs a process and its children on the M1's efficiency cores (copy 1 KiB at 7M per second instead of 27M); devtool and the suite leave it, and warn of a background QoS clamp they can't lift.

Go's calls go into the library through purego, each behind a mutex of its direction: that costs more than copying a short message, so copied 16-byte messages run at about a quarter of Rust's rate on Windows and a fifth of it on Linux. Zero-copy takes two calls per message on each side where a copy takes one, so in Go it runs slower than copying for short messages; from 64 KiB on, Go runs as fast as the other languages.

C#'s object layer (FipcConnection) adds about 30 ns per message over the raw layer on both OSes, nearly all of it the SafeHandle's reference count: for 16-byte copies, 80 ns against 42 ns per send and receive on Windows, 72 ns against 38 ns on Linux, in one process with no waiting.

Machine and method

two machines
  • The Linux and Windows columns: an ASUS Zenbook 14 (UX3404VC) laptop on AC power, Windows power plan "High performance".
  • Intel Core i9-13900H: 6 P-cores with Hyper-Threading and 8 E-cores, 20 logical CPUs; 32 GB RAM.
  • Windows 11 Pro 10.0.26200. WSL2 Ubuntu 24.04.3 with glibc 2.39 and 20 vCPUs.
  • No CPU pinning. IDEs, a browser and background processes idle alongside; Windows and WSL ran one after the other.
  • The macOS* column, a different machine: a MacBook Air (M1, 4 performance and 4 efficiency cores, 8 GB RAM), macOS 26.5.1, on power; ReleaseFast for Apple M1, unpinned. Medians of devtool bench-compare --ab --runs 5 --duration 1 on 2026-10-07: 5 alternating rounds of 1 s per case against the library without the stream pace.
  • The † Linux VM column: the same MacBook Air, Linux in a Lima 2.2.1 VM (Apple Virtualization framework): Ubuntu 24.04.5 arm64, kernel 6.8, glibc 2.39, 4 vCPUs, 4 GB; ReleaseFast for generic ARMv8.0-A, unpinned; the same A/B as the macOS* column, 2026-10-07. The languages' Linux VM columns: everything built the same way, 5 runs round-robin of each benchmark with Node.js 22.23.3 and Go 1.27.1, the Mac otherwise idle, 2026-10-07.
  • On the M1, under macOS and Linux alike, the timer ticks at 24 MHz (about 42 ns), so latencies are quantized to that: 167 ns is 4 ticks. Its cache line for the lines that matter is 128 bytes, so a ring's head and tail indices, 64 bytes apart, share one.
  • The library table's Linux and Windows columns are an A/B run: 5 alternating rounds of 0.3 s per case, medians; its macOS* and Linux VM columns ran 1 s per case. The suite's own runs, below, are longer.
  • Throughput: after a 0.2 s warm-up, 5 runs of about 1 s of steady traffic; every message's length is checked, and zero-copy receivers copy each message out.
  • Latency: 5 runs of 1 s of samples on the CPU's time-stamp counter (on Apple Silicon, the 24 MHz virtual counter); a round trip minus the peer's hold time.
  • Setup: 5 runs of 1 s of whole connection lives.
  • Reproduce with python devtool.py bench zig-all, or bench-compare --ab for an A/B against another revision.
